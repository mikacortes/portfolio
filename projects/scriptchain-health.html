Overview

I was the sole person responsible for ScriptChain Health's social media presence across Instagram, LinkedIn, and TikTok for six months.

ScriptChain Health is an early-stage health tech startup building an agentic AI platform that uses food and exercise as medicine to treat metabolic syndrome and reduce hospital readmissions. With a small but ambitious team, the company was working toward its first major product launch while trying to establish credibility with healthcare stakeholders, potential users, and investors.

My role included social media content strategy, graphic design, video production, newsletter creation, and marketing collateral design.

The Challenge

The accounts had been dormant for months, and there was no strategy, brand guidance, or handoff documentation waiting for me.

Instagram and LinkedIn hadn't seen activity since August 2025, and TikTok since February. The role had been described as designing posts, but researching topics, developing a voice, building a content strategy, and managing scheduling all fell to me as well. The first two posts I published were built by studying the most recent uploads and replacing the content with new research, a deliberate starting point while I got my footing and figured out what the brand needed to say and to whom.

Finding Direction

A conversation about an upcoming company event made me realize social media needed to market ScriptChain, not just educate the internet.

When the CEO mentioned a collaborative research study being presented at an upcoming event, it reframed how I was thinking about the channels entirely. I had been posting solid healthcare research, but the accounts weren't doing anything to position ScriptChain specifically. From that point forward, I balanced educational content around metabolic syndrome and AI in healthcare with posts that spoke directly to who ScriptChain is, what makes the platform meaningful, and where the company was showing up in the world.

Building the Strategy

I researched competitors, tracked our own performance data, and turned those findings into a documented content strategy I handed off to the company.

With no existing playbook, I studied how similar health tech companies used social media, looking at post types, engagement hooks, and how they balanced promotional and educational content. Alongside that, I tracked our own metrics closely, testing days and times, comparing video versus static performance, and noting which topics and formats resonated most on each platform. That research shaped a formal strategy covering audience definitions and goals by platform, a weekly posting cadence, platform-specific technical guidance, and newsletter best practices. It was designed to be picked up and built on by whoever came next.

The Work

Over six months, I produced 78 posts across three platforms while also creating newsletters, event materials, a brochure, and investor presentations.

  • 28 Instagram posts
  • 26 LinkedIn posts
  • 24 TikTok posts

Content ranged from metabolic syndrome research and AI in healthcare to event announcements, study collaborations, holiday posts, and culturally timely content. I also built a social media visual identity from scratch, developing a branding system based on the product's existing color palette since none existed for the channels before.

Outside of social media, I contributed newsletters via beehiiv, event posters, a tri-fold brochure, podcast outreach on behalf of the CEO, and investor and speaking presentations that the company continues to use today.

What the Data Showed

Tracking performance across all three platforms revealed clear patterns in what formats, days, and content types actually moved the needle.

From the start, I logged every post across Instagram, LinkedIn, and TikTok — tracking views, reactions, content type, day, and time. Over time, patterns emerged that informed how I scheduled and formatted content going forward.

On LinkedIn, carousels and static graphics posted on Tuesday or Thursday mornings consistently outperformed other formats and times, averaging 60 to 85 views during my tenure. Video on LinkedIn trended lower in raw views but held its own in reactions. Posts from before my start date averaged significantly higher views (often 100 to 260+), which reflected a larger or more active follower base at the time — a useful benchmark to work back toward.

On Instagram, video posts were the clearest standout. The highest single-post view count on the platform during my time was 192 views on a Tuesday video, and video consistently outperformed static graphics across the board. Static posts clustered in the 25 to 65 view range, while video regularly pushed past that. Reactions stayed fairly steady across formats at 2 to 5 per post, suggesting the audience was small but consistent.

TikTok told the most interesting story. Every post prior to my joining had received zero likes. The six posts I published during my internship all received at least one like, with the top performer — the Olympics post — reaching 56 likes and 1,421 views. Even the lower-performing TikToks in my batch hit 86 to 144 views, signaling that simply resuming activity on a dormant account had a measurable effect.

Across all three platforms, I used these observations to refine the posting schedule, prioritize video content, and experiment with timing. That ongoing tracking also fed directly into the strategy document, so the next person wouldn't have to start from zero.

Highlight: The Olympics Post

A single timely post became the most-liked in the TikTok account's history, with 56 likes and 1,421 views against a baseline of 3 to 4 likes and 100 to 500 views per post.

Near the end of my internship, I leaned further into culturally relevant content as part of the strategy. The 2026 Winter Olympics post was the result of that thinking, and the response confirmed what the research had been pointing to: audiences engage more when content connects to something they are already paying attention to, even in a niche healthcare context.

Reflections

The most valuable thing I built at ScriptChain was not any individual post, but the ability to think about social media as a strategic tool rather than a design task.

Stepping into a role with no brief and no precedent pushed me to develop skills I would not have had the chance to use otherwise: market research, content planning, performance analysis, and the discipline to build a strategy before touching a design file. The accounts did not go viral, and for a pre-launch startup targeting a niche healthcare audience, that was never a realistic short-term goal. What this period accomplished was keeping the channels credible and active, establishing a visual identity, and giving the company a foundation to grow from. That work continued to matter after I left.

Beyond Social Media

Alongside the social channels, I designed physical marketing materials, investor presentations, and a newsletter program that extended ScriptChain's presence beyond the screen.

For events where the CEO presented or hosted a table, I designed an informational poster and a tri-fold brochure that introduced ScriptChain to attendees, covering the platform, its mission, and the problem it solves. These needed to communicate clearly to a healthcare audience that may have been encountering the company for the first time, so the design prioritized clarity and credibility over flash.

I also designed presentations for both speaking engagements and investor meetings. Those decks are still actively in use by the company today.

On the newsletter side, I set up and sent campaigns through beehiiv with an eye toward what actually gets emails opened and read. That meant writing subject lines built to perform, designing mobile-responsive layouts, maintaining a clean visual hierarchy, and keeping calls to action focused. I also researched newsletter best practices around open rates, click-to-open rates, send frequency, and list hygiene to inform how the program should grow beyond my time there.

Wishes

Given more bandwidth and platform flexibility, I would have pushed harder to expand our reach, particularly onto Facebook.

Because social media was one part of a broader design role running on a 20-hour week, there was a ceiling on how much I could realistically produce and experiment with. I made the most of the time available, but more runway would have meant more testing, more content variation, and a faster feedback loop on what was working.

The bigger strategic wish was getting the company onto Facebook. Given that ScriptChain's target users skew older, Facebook's demographic made it a natural fit, and I made that case to the CEO. It didn't move forward, and the three-channel limit on Buffer's free plan meant there wasn't room to add it anyway without dropping something else. Beyond platform access, Buffer itself introduced a few friction points worth noting for anyone continuing the work: music couldn't be added to TikTok posts through the scheduler, and gradient designs didn't render correctly when uploading PDFs for LinkedIn carousels. Small constraints, but the kind that quietly shape what's possible.

Learnings

How to prioritize, context-switch, and own work that wasn't in the original job description.

I had worked in the startup environment before, but this role pushed that experience further. Being expected to research content, develop strategy, design across multiple formats, write newsletters, build presentations, and handle outreach within a part-time schedule meant learning quickly how to triage and keep moving without losing quality. That kind of stretched responsibility is uncomfortable, but it builds a different kind of confidence than a well-scoped role does.

This was also my first time working on a healthcare product, and the subject matter ended up being genuinely interesting.

Going in, I knew very little about metabolic syndrome or how AI was being applied in preventive care. The research I did for posts, combined with the CEO's expectation that the team stay informed on the space, meant I was absorbing a lot more than I expected to. I came out of it with a working understanding of B2B healthcare dynamics, some awareness of how compliance and trust factor into health tech marketing, and a few genuinely useful health insights I've kept for myself.

Final Mentions

This internship was as much a lesson in how I want to work as it was in the work itself.

Beyond the deliverables, my time at ScriptChain gave me a clearer picture of the types of projects I want to keep pursuing and how to stay grounded when the scope of a role expands faster than the hours allow. That kind of self-knowledge is hard to come by without being thrown into the deep end.

A genuine thank you to the ScriptChain team for the experience. And a special shoutout to my fellow Product Designer and the engineer who kept me sane throughout — their feedback, perspective, and camaraderie made the harder stretches a lot more manageable.
